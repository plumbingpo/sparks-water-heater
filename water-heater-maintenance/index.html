The inside of a neglected water heater tells the whole story. On the bottom there is a bed of mineral sediment, sometimes several inches deep, that makes the burner work harder, creates popping and rumbling, and can burn out the lower element on an electric unit. Hanging from the top is the anode rod, meant to corrode first, which may be down to bare wire after a few years. When it is used up, corrosion goes after the tank lining, and a leak is only a matter of time. Across Sparks, NV, a yearly visit is the simplest way to keep both of those problems from shortening the life of the heater.

We work through a full checklist every time. The tank is drained and flushed to clear the sediment layer. The anode rod comes out for inspection and is replaced when little of it remains. The T&P relief valve is tested so we know it will open under pressure and close again cleanly. On gas models, we check the burner, pilot assembly, thermocouple or flame sensor, flue, and draft. Electric heaters get element and thermostat testing and a check of the wiring connections. We also measure the air charge in the expansion tank, check the water pressure, set the thermostat around 120 degrees unless a mixing valve allows higher storage, and inspect the drain valve, connections, and pan.

Tankless owners need maintenance too, just a different kind. Scale builds up inside the heat exchanger, especially in hard water, and eventually triggers error codes, reduced flow, or temperature swings. We flush the heat exchanger with a pump and descaling solution through the service valves, clean the inlet filter, check the venting and condensate line on condensing models, and review the unit's error history. When to Call for Water Heater Maintenance

Water heaters usually give plenty of warning before they fail, as long as you know what to look and listen for. Watch for these telltale signs that professional help is needed:

  • A tankless unit showing error codes or fluctuating temperatures
  • Noises from the water heater that were not there before
  • Hot water running out sooner than it used to
  • Hot water that looks cloudy or rust-colored
  • Higher gas or electric costs for the same routine
  • A water heater that has never been flushed or inspected
  • A rotten-egg smell from the hot water
  • Small drips at the drain valve or connections

The Benefits of Professional Water Heater Maintenance

There is a short list of things you can do yourself: watching for leaks, keeping the area around the heater clear, and noting new noises. Beyond that, the work gets riskier. Draining a tank means handling scalding water and a drain valve that may not close again if it is old and full of sediment. Pulling an anode rod often takes a breaker bar and real leverage on a fitting that has not moved in years. Testing a T&P valve on an older heater can leave it dripping afterward. On gas units, the burner, thermocouple, and venting involve combustion safety, and a vent that is not drafting can put carbon monoxide into the house.

Professional maintenance also brings the right parts on the truck, including drain valves, anode rods and flexible anodes, relief valves, and meters for electrical testing. Tankless descaling gear comes along for those units. Experience counts as much as equipment: an experienced eye can spot a corroding nipple, a sooty burner, or an expansion tank that has lost its charge well before any of them cause trouble. Sparks Water Heater treats maintenance as a way to catch small issues early and keep your heater working for as many years as possible.

How to Prevent Water Heater Problems

Most of the failures we see could have been delayed for years with basic upkeep. Here are our recommendations:

  • Flush annually, more often where the water is hard
  • Set the water temperature around 120 degrees for safety and efficiency
  • Look around the base of the heater periodically for moisture
  • Leave open space around the tank, especially near a gas burner
  • Treat hard water to cut down on sediment and scale
  • Descale tankless units once a year, or more often in hard water
  • Have the anode rod checked every few years and replaced when it is mostly gone
